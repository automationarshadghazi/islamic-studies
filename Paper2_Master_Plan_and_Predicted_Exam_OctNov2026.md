## ✅ `Question 1(a)` — `[5 / 5 Marks]` *(Time taken: 3.5 minutes!)*
> In Islamic law (*shariah*), valid divorce (*talaq*) involves strict conditions and safeguards rather than impulsive separation. First, prior reconciliation must be attempted, including family arbitration with one mediator from the husband’s family and one from the wife’s family (**Qurʾan 4:35**). Second, the husband pronouncing *talaq* must be adult, sane, and acting with conscious intent. Third, according to the *Sunnah* method (*talaq al-sunnah*), a single pronouncement should only be made during a period of purity (*tuhr*) after menstruation in which no marital relations have occurred (**Qurʾan 65:1**). Fourth, following pronouncement, the wife observes a mandatory waiting period (*ʿiddah*) of three menstrual cycles (*quruʾ*, **Qurʾan 2:228**) inside the marital home supported financially by the husband, allowing reconciliation (*rujuʿ*) before the divorce becomes final. Fifth, in Twelver Shiʾi jurisprudence (*Jaʿfari*), two just male witnesses (*ʿadulan*) must hear the exact Arabic formula of divorce for it to be valid. Sixth, a woman also holds the legal right to initiate divorce (*khulʿ* or judicial dissolution *faskh*) before a *qadi* on grounds of harm or incompatibility (**Qurʾan 2:229**).

## ✅ `Question 1(b)` — `[10 / 10 Marks]` *(Named School in Sentence 1: **Shafiʿi** [or **Hanafi** noted!])*
> According to the **Shafiʿi school of Islamic jurisprudence (*fiqh*)**, founded by **Imam Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafiʿi**, divorce (*talaq*) is legally permissible as a last resort when marital harmony collapses, yet strongly discouraged (*makruh*) unless genuine harm exists, reflecting the Hadith: *"Of all lawful things, divorce is the most disliked by Allah"* [Abu Dawud]. Shafiʿi rulings strictly balance *Qurʾanic* verses (**Qurʾan 2:228–230**, **65:1–2**) with authentic *Sunnah* reports to protect family stability and women’s financial rights.
>
> First, Shafiʿi *fiqh* distinguishes between **Sunnah-compliant divorce (*talaq sunni*)** and **irregular/innovated divorce (*talaq bidʿi*)**. For a proper *talaq sunni*, the husband must be sane, adult, and uncoerced, and must pronounce **one single revocable divorce (*talaq rajʿi*)** while his wife is in a state of post-menstrual purity (*tuhr*) during which no sexual intercourse has taken place. Pronouncing divorce during menstruation is sinful (*haram*) in Shafiʿi *fiqh* because it unfairly prolongs the wife’s waiting period, echoing the Prophet’s rebuke of ʿAbdullah ibn ʿUmar when he divorced his wife during her menses [Bukhari].
>
> Second, during the mandatory **three-purity waiting period (*ʿiddah*, Qurʾan 2:228)** after a first or second pronouncement, the marriage is not yet severed: the husband remains legally obligated to provide full housing and maintenance (*nafaqah*), cannot expel her from the home (**Qurʾan 65:1**), and may revoke the divorce (*rujuʿ*) verbally and reunite with kindness. If the *ʿiddah* expires without reconciliation, the divorce becomes minorly irrevocable (*baʾin baynunah sughra*), requiring a fresh marriage contract (*nikah*) and new dower (*mahr*) if they later wish to remarry.
>
> Third, regarding **triple divorce (*talaq al-thalath*)**, classical Shafiʿi (alongside Hanafi) jurists rule that even if three divorces are pronounced in one sitting, though spiritually blameworthy, all three legally take effect as a **major irrevocable divorce (*baʾin baynunah kubra*)**, barring remarriage unless the woman genuinely marries another man and that second marriage ends naturally (**Qurʾan 2:230**). Finally, Shafiʿi law safeguards women through **bride-initiated release (*khulʿ*, Qurʾan 2:229)**—modelled on Thabit ibn Qays’s wife returning her garden in the Prophet’s time [Bukhari]—and **judicial annulment (*faskh*)** granted by an Islamic judge (*qadi*) if a husband fails to pay maintenance, abandons her, or inflicts cruelty, ensuring women keep every penny of deferred *mahr* when wronged.

## ✅ `Question 1(c)` — `[10 / 10 Marks]` *(Level 5 `D — A — C — W` Structure Referencing Two Schools!)*
> Divorce (*talaq*) dissolves the sacred covenant of *nikah*. Whether *shariah* makes divorce **far too easy for men and unfair for women** is fiercely debated across classical and contemporary *fiqh*, particularly when comparing **traditional Sunni schools (such as Hanafi)** with **Twelver Shiʾi (*Jaʿfari*) and reformist interpretations**.
>
> **Arguments Supporting the Statement (Where Male Advantage & Practical Abuse Exist):**  
> Strong criticism arises primarily from classical **Hanafi** rules on unilateral male pronouncement. In traditional Hanafi *fiqh*, a husband requires **neither a judge (*qadi*), witnesses, nor his wife’s consent** to pronounce valid *talaq*; worse still, classical Hanafi jurists count even a *talaq* uttered in anger, jest, or intoxication—and an instant **"triple *talaq*"** shouted three times in one breath—as legally binding and irrevocably final. This can leave a wife homeless and divorced overnight without mediation, which critics rightly argue contradicts the *Qurʾanic* spirit of deliberation. By contrast, a woman seeking *khulʿ* often must forfeit her *mahr* financially or face slow, male-dominated *shariah* councils when proving harm for judicial *faskh*.
>
> **Counter-Arguments (Strong Protections for Women Across *Jaʿfari* Shiʾi & Sunni/Reformist Law):**  
> Conversely, describing *shariah* itself as unfair overlooks profound legal checks across other schools. **Twelver Shiʾi (*Jaʿfari*) *fiqh*** rejects triple *talaq* in one sitting (counting it as at most one or invalid), strictly invalidates any divorce spoken in extreme anger, intoxication, or during menstruation, and mandates **two upright witnesses** (`Q 65:2`), drastically curbing impulsive male divorce. Furthermore, **Ibn Taymiyyah**, **Salafi scholars**, and modern family codes in Egypt, Pakistan, and Morocco count triple *talaq* in one sitting as **one single revocable divorce**, restoring the *Qurʾanic* three-month *ʿiddah* buffer (`Q 2:228`). Financially, divorce is actually costly—not cheap—for men: the husband immediately owes all unpaid deferred *mahr*, full housing and *nafaqah* during *ʿiddah*, consolatory maintenance (*mutʿat al-talaq*, **Qurʾan 2:241**), and 100% ongoing child maintenance, while both Hanafi and Shafiʿi contracts allow women to insert **delegated divorce rights (*talaq al-tafwid*)** directly into their *nikahnama*!
>
> **Evaluative Level 5 Conclusion:**  
> Overall, the statement is **partially convincing when aimed at un-reformed cultural abuses of classical Hanafi triple *talaq***, which genuinely allow rash male unilateralism. However, as an indictment of *shariah* as a whole, it is **overstated**: when **Qurʾan 4:35** arbitration, **Jaʿfari** witness requirements, delegated marriage-contract clauses (*tafwid*), judicial *faskh*, and mandatory male financial obligations are properly enforced together, Islamic law makes divorce seriously accountable for men while protecting women’s dignity and economic security.

## ✅ `Question 3(a)` — `[10 / 10 Marks]` *(Unit `2.9` Modernist / Reformist Approaches)*
> **Modernist (or reformist) Islam** emerged from the nineteenth century onwards as Muslim thinkers sought to revitalise Islamic civilisation amidst Western colonialism, scientific progress, and social change. Thinkers such as **Sir Sayyid Ahmad Khan** (1817–1898) in South Asia, **Muhammad ʿAbduh** (1849–1905) at Al-Azhar in Egypt, and twentieth-century scholar **Fazlur Rahman** (1919–1988) argue that Islam’s eternal ethical principles must be dynamically re-applied to modern realities rather than frozen in medieval legal manuals.
>
> First, reformists champion **re-opening the gates of independent reasoning (*ijtihad*)** and rejecting blind imitation (*taqlid*) of medieval *madhhabs*. **Muhammad ʿAbduh** argued that **reason (*ʿaql*) and revelation (*wahy*) can never genuinely conflict** because both come from One All-Wise Creator; when apparent tension arises with proven science, scripture was misunderstood and requires rational re-interpretation. Similarly, **Sir Sayyid Ahmad Khan**, who founded Aligarh Muslim University, urged Muslims to embrace modern empirical science, English education, and women’s schooling for social advancement.
>
> Second, modernists distinguish sharply between **unchanging eternal worship/creed (*ʿibadat* & *tawhid*)** and **time-bound social laws (*muʿamalat*)**. **Fazlur Rahman’s "Double Movement Theory"** illustrates this method clearly: first, a scholar moves back to 7th-century Mecca and Medina to identify the **underlying moral objective (*hikmah / maqsad*)** behind a specific *Qurʾanic* ruling; second, the scholar brings that universal moral principle forward into today’s social context.
>
> For example, modernists read **Qurʾan 4:3** on polygyny alongside **Qurʾan 4:129** (*"You will never be able to be completely fair between wives"*) to argue the *Qurʾan’s* original trajectory moved society from unlimited *Jahiliyyah* polygyny toward **monogamy**, inspiring legal reforms in countries like Tunisia. Likewise, some reformists distinguish exploitative pre-Islamic loan-shark usury (*riba*, **Qurʾan 3:130** *doubled and multiplied*) from regulated modern bank interest, advocate gender equality in education and public leadership, and harmonise *shariah* objectives (*maqasid al-shariah*) with constitutional democracy and universal human rights.

## ✅ `Question 3(b)` — `[15 / 15 Marks]` *(Level 5 `D — S — D — J` Comparison: **Modern Salafis vs Modernist Reformists**)*
> Both **modern Salafism**—rooted in the thought of **Taqi al-Din Ibn Taymiyyah** (d. 1328), **Muhammad ibn ʿAbd al-Wahhab** (d. 1792), and modern Hadith scholars—and **Islamic modernism/reformism**—led by figures such as **Muhammad ʿAbduh**, **Sir Sayyid Ahmad Khan**, and **Fazlur Rahman**—arose as revivalist movements seeking to cure Muslim decline by returning directly to Islam’s foundational sources. Comparing the two reveals striking shared starting points alongside fundamentally opposite interpretive destinations.
>
> **Explicit Similarities (Shared Rejection of Medieval Stagnation & Superstition):**  
> Surprisingly, both movements share **three major foundational positions**. First, **both uphold the supreme authority of the *Qurʾan* as the Word of God and *Tawhid* as the core of Islam**, rejecting shrine worship, saint intercession (*tawassul* at graves), amulets, and superstitious folk innovations (*bidʿah*) that crept into popular Sufism—indeed, early modernist **Muhammad ʿAbduh** himself praised returning to the pure faith of the early ancestors (*al-Salaf al-Salih*). Second, **both movements critique rigid, unquestioning blind imitation (*taqlid*) of the four classical medieval *madhhabs***, insisting that contemporary Muslims must engage directly with the primary texts through **active *ijtihad*** rather than assuming medieval scholars answered every question forever. Third, both movements view Islam as actively relevant to reforming society and education rather than retreating from the world.
>
> **Explicit Differences (Literal Textualism vs Contextual Rationalism):**  
> Despite sharing the vocabulary of *"returning to primary sources"* and *"ijtihad"*, their **methodologies and conclusions are diametrically opposed**. First, in **scriptural hermeneutics**, **Salafis insist on strict literal fidelity (*zahir*) to explicit *Qurʾanic* texts, authentic (*sahih*) Hadith, and the consensus/practice of the *Sahaba* and first three generations (*Salaf*)**, warning that bending texts to modern fashion distorts Allah’s unchanging command (**Qurʾan 5:3**). Conversely, **modernist reformists elevate human reason (*ʿaql*), historical contextualisation (*asbab al-nuzul*), and higher ethical purposes (*maqasid*)**, viewing 7th-century Medinan rulings on criminal penalties (*hudud*), polygyny, or testimony as historical stepping-stones whose spirit—justice and human dignity—must be freshly applied today.
>
> Second, regarding **Hadith and theology**, Salafis treat every authenticated *sahih* Hadith in Bukhari and Muslim as binding law alongside the *Qurʾan* and affirm divine attributes literally without likeness (*bila tashbih*), whereas modernists like **Sir Sayyid Ahmad Khan** and **Fazlur Rahman** subject solitary (*ahad*) Hadiths to historical-rational scrutiny when they appear to clash with *Qurʾanic* egalitarianism or science, interpreting miracles and anthropomorphic verses metaphorically. Practically, this produces opposite rulings across everyday life—from gender roles, dress, and *Mawlid* to banking mortgages and interfaith relations.
>
> **Evaluative Level 5 Conclusion (Judged Extent):**  
> In conclusion, **the methodological and practical differences between modern Salafism and modernist reformism far outweigh their surface similarities**. Although both begin at the exact same doorway—bypassing medieval *taqlid* to return to the *Qurʾan* and the early community—they walk in **opposite directions**: **Salafism pulls modern society back to conform literally to the 7th-century model of the *Salaf*** to safeguard religious purity against corruption, whereas **modernism carries 7th-century *Qurʾanic* moral principles forward into modern society** through rational *ijtihad* to ensure Islam thrives dynamically in every era.
